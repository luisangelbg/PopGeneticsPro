What population genetics actually asks start here

A species is rarely one big interbreeding unit. It is a set of populations that exchange genes at some rate, lose variation at some rate, and accumulate differences at some rate. Population genetics measures those three rates from a sample of individuals, and asks four questions in this order:

  1. How much genetic variation is there, and is it the same in every population? (Block 3)
  2. Is that variation organised the way random mating predicts, or is there inbreeding, selfing, clonality or a scoring problem? (Block 4)
  3. How is the variation distributed — mostly within populations, or between them? (Blocks 5–7)
  4. What history produced that pattern — gene flow, isolation, a bottleneck, an expansion? (Block 9)
The one number to remember In most outcrossing plants, 80–95% of the genetic variation sits within populations and only 5–20% between them. In selfers the proportions often reverse. Knowing which kind of species you have changes how you read every result that follows.
Hardy–Weinberg: the null model everything is measured against Block 4
Genotype frequencies under random mating

With two alleles at frequencies p and q, random mating in a large population with no selection, mutation or migration gives genotype frequencies p², 2pq and q² — and they stay there, generation after generation. That is the Hardy–Weinberg principle, and it is useful precisely because real populations depart from it.

expected heterozygosity He = 1 − Σ pᵢ² observed heterozygosity Ho = (number of heterozygotes) / N fixation index F = (He − Ho) / He

A positive F (fewer heterozygotes than expected) is the everyday result in plants, and it has several possible causes that the analysis must separate:

  • Selfing or biparental inbreeding — the biological explanation, consistent across all loci.
  • The Wahlund effect — you pooled two genetically distinct groups into one "population".
  • Null alleles — a primer site mutation hides one allele, so true heterozygotes are scored as homozygotes. Affects one locus, not all of them.
How to tell them apart Inbreeding raises F at every locus by a similar amount; a null allele raises it at one locus only. Block 4 reports FIS per locus and per population side by side for exactly this reason.
The four forces: drift, migration, mutation, selection simulator
Independent populations drifting apart

Drift is the random sampling of gametes between generations. It is not a small correction: in a population of effective size Ne, heterozygosity decays as Ht = H0(1 − 1/2Ne)t, and alleles are eventually lost or fixed by chance alone. Small and fragmented populations lose variation fast.

Migration pushes the other way. In Wright's island model the equilibrium differentiation is FST ≈ 1/(1 + 4Nem), which is why one successful migrant per generation (Nem = 1) is enough to keep populations from diverging by drift.

Mutation creates new variation, slowly (10⁻⁵–10⁻³ per locus per generation for microsatellites, much lower for sequences). Selection changes frequencies systematically, but only at the loci it acts on — which is what makes neutral markers useful for reading demography.

Read Nem with care The conversion of FST into a number of migrants assumes an island model at equilibrium, with no selection and no mutation. Report it as an indicative value, never as a migration count.
F-statistics and AMOVA: where the variation sits Block 5
Pairwise differentiation between populations

Wright's three F-statistics describe the same sample at three levels: FIS compares an individual with its own population (inbreeding), FST compares populations with the total (differentiation), and FIT compares an individual with the total.

G_ST = (H_T − H_S) / H_T Nei (1973) θ (theta) Weir & Cockerham (1984), the estimator to report G'_ST = G_ST / G_ST(max) Hedrick (2005), corrected for high diversity D = (H_T − H_S)/(1 − H_S) · k/(k−1) Jost (2008), "true" differentiation

With highly polymorphic markers such as microsatellites, HS can be so high that GST is mathematically capped far below 1 — two populations sharing no alleles at all may still give GST = 0.1. That is the reason to report G′ST or Jost's D alongside it, not instead of it.

AMOVA (Excoffier et al. 1992) does the same job as an analysis of variance on a distance matrix, and it is the right tool when your design is hierarchical: individuals within populations, within regions. It gives a percentage of variation at each level and tests each one by permutation — and because it works from distances, it handles dominant markers and haplotypes as easily as microsatellites.

Dominant markers: what AFLP and ISSR can and cannot tell you binary data

A dominant band is present or absent. The heterozygote looks exactly like the dominant homozygote, so Ho cannot be observed and FIS cannot be estimated from the data alone. Allele frequencies must be inferred from the frequency of the null phenotype:

q = √(freq of band-absent) assuming Hardy-Weinberg Lynch & Milligan (1994) small-sample correction Bayesian estimate with a prior on F when HWE cannot be assumed
  • Analyses that still work: ΦPT and AMOVA, Nei's gene diversity, Jaccard/Dice distances, PCoA, Bayesian clustering, Mantel tests, spatial autocorrelation.
  • Analyses that do not: observed heterozygosity, HWE tests, FIS, assignment based on genotype likelihoods, most bottleneck tests.
Two things to do before analysing bands Score the same gel twice and report the error rate, and drop bands present in fewer than ~5% or more than ~95% of individuals. Block 2 does both checks for you.
Plants are different: selfing, clones, pollen and seed plant-specific
Mating system: outcrossing, selfing and pollen flow

Most population genetics theory was written for animals that mate at random and move as adults. Plants do neither, and four consequences show up in every dataset:

  • Mixed mating. Outcrossing rates (tm) range from near 0 to near 1, often within the same species. Selfing reduces Ne, inflates FIS and speeds up differentiation between populations.
  • Clonality. Repeated multilocus genotypes must be detected before anything else — a clone counted as 40 individuals will distort every index. Block 2 flags them.
  • Two dispersal vehicles. Pollen carries nuclear genes, seed carries everything. Comparing nuclear with maternally inherited chloroplast markers separates the two, and their differentiation ratio estimates the pollen-to-seed flow ratio.
  • Fine-scale spatial structure. Limited seed dispersal makes neighbours relatives. That is what spatial autocorrelation and the Sp statistic quantify (Block 9) — and it is why a "population" sampled along a transect may not be one population at all.

      2 · What the tests say

      3 · Hardy–Weinberg tests, locus by population

      The exact test conditions on the allele counts and asks how unusual the observed genotype table is (Guo & Thompson 1992, by Monte Carlo). The chi-square test is shown for comparison with older papers; it is unreliable whenever an expected count falls below five, which with microsatellites is almost always.

      4 · Inbreeding coefficient FIS

      f is Weir & Cockerham's (1984) estimator, multilocus as a ratio of summed variance components, with a bootstrap interval and a jackknife error over loci; the mean of Nei & Chesser's (1983) single-locus values is given for comparison. The selfing rate is the equilibrium value implied by f, s = 2F/(1+F) — a reference, not a measurement.

      Locus by locus: the diagnostic

      Inbreeding raises FIS at every locus by a similar amount. A null allele raises it at one locus, in most populations. A locus is flagged when it shows a significant deficit in at least half the populations and its estimated null frequency exceeds 5%.

      5 · Null alleles

      6 · Linkage disequilibrium

      The index of association measures whether individuals that differ at one locus tend to differ at the others. r̄d (Agapow & Burt 2001) is its version corrected for the number of loci, so it can be compared between studies. It works for codominant, dominant and haploid data alike, and is tested by shuffling each locus independently among individuals. In an outcrossing plant with unlinked markers it is close to zero; selfing, clonality and admixture push it up.

      5 · Principal coordinates

      PCoA (Gower 1966) turns the distance matrix into a map in which the distances between points reproduce the genetic distances as well as a plane can. Unlike a PCA of allele frequencies it works with any coefficient, which is why it is the standard ordination in population genetics.

      6 · Trees

      Neighbour-joining (Saitou & Nei 1987) builds an unrooted tree without assuming equal rates; UPGMA assumes them and gives a rooted, ultrametric tree. Support values are the percentage of bootstrap resamplings of the loci (bands, or alignment sites for sequences) in which the same grouping reappears (Felsenstein 1985) — groupings under 70% are not to be interpreted. Trees of individuals get support when there are 60 or fewer.

      DNA sequences & haplotypes

      What an alignment says about diversity, demographic history and the geography of lineages.

      1 · Settings

      Turns τ into years since expansion. Chloroplast non-coding DNA in angiosperms is often quoted at 1–3 × 10⁻⁹.

      2 · What the sequences say

      3 · Sites

      4 · Haplotypes

      5 · Diversity and neutrality tests

      Tajima's D compares two estimates of θ — one from the number of segregating sites, one from pairwise differences — that agree only under neutrality and constant size. Fu's Fs asks whether there are more haplotypes than expected given π; it is the most sensitive to population growth. Fu & Li's D* and F* weigh the singletons; R₂ (Ramos-Onsins & Rozas 2002) is the most powerful for small samples. All P-values come from coalescent simulations of a neutral population of constant size, not from tables; they assume at most one mutation per site.

      Mismatch distribution

      The distribution of pairwise differences is unimodal after a sudden expansion (Rogers & Harpending 1992) and ragged in a population of stable size; the raggedness r (Harpending 1994) measures it. τ = 2ut dates the expansion in mutational units.

      6 · Structure among populations

      Pons & Petit (1996): if NST, which weights haplotypes by their divergence, is significantly larger than GST, which treats every haplotype as equally different, then related haplotypes share populations — the lineages are geographically sorted.

      7 · Haplotype network

      Median-joining (Bandelt, Forster & Röhl 1999) with ε = 0: the minimum spanning network of the haplotypes, completed with the unsampled intermediates (median vectors) of the lowest connection cost. Node area is proportional to frequency, slices show the populations, and each tick on a link is one mutation.

      8 · Divergence times — a dated tree

      A molecular clock turns substitutions into time. Something external has to set the clock: fossils or published ages for nodes of a tree of species or genera, or a known substitution rate for haplotypes within one species, where fossils almost never apply. Two methods are offered: a fast least-squares dating (LSD; To et al. 2016) to explore, and a Bayesian relaxed-clock MCMC (Drummond et al. 2006) that gives a 95% credible interval for every node.

      2

      What sets the clock

      How a fossil calibrates a node. A fossil that can be placed in a clade proves the clade existed by the time the rock formed, so it gives a minimum age — for the crown node when it has features of a living subgroup, for the stem node (the branch below the clade) when it only belongs to the lineage. Use the younger boundary of the stratigraphic unit as the minimum. A maximum is rarely known: it comes from the absence of the group in older, well-sampled rocks, and is expressed as a soft upper tail. A secondary calibration takes a node age from a published dated tree, with its uncertainty.
      Geological ages: .
      Rates vary among lineages and with generation time: use one estimated for your group when it exists.

      Per million years = per year × 10⁶: a rate of 2 × 10⁻⁹ per site per year is 0.002 per site per Myr. Ages then come out in millions of years (Ma); for recent intraspecific histories, read 0.01 Ma as 10 000 years.

      Demography & spatial structure

      Has the population crashed, how large is it genetically, are neighbours relatives, how much does it self, and is trait divergence more than drift?

      1 · Settings

      IAM   TPM   SMM
      Piry et al. (1999) recommend the TPM with 95% single-step mutations and variance 12 for microsatellites. Needs ≥ 10 plants per population.
      Pcrit = 0.02 is the usual choice (Waples & Do 2010); rare alleles bias r² upward.
      Classes hold equal numbers of pairs, so each estimate has similar precision. Pairs within populations measure fine-scale structure; all pairs mix in the differentiation among populations.
      c is the fraction of the between-population phenotypic variance that is genetic; h² the heritability within. c/h² = 1 is the most optimistic assumption.

      2 · What the analyses say

      3 · Recent bottlenecks

      Cornuet & Luikart (1996): after a bottleneck, rare alleles disappear faster than heterozygosity declines, so for a while each locus shows more heterozygosity than its number of alleles predicts at mutation–drift equilibrium. That expectation is obtained here by coalescent simulation conditioned on the observed number of alleles (Piry, Luikart & Cornuet 1999). The distribution of He given the number of alleles is skewed, so the Wilcoxon test is applied to the position of each locus within its own equilibrium distribution (as a normal score), and with inbreeding the equilibrium is simulated for the n(2 − F) independent gene copies that n plants carry. Luikart's mode shift and the M-ratio of Garza & Williamson look at the same history from other angles.

      Locus by locus

      4 · Effective population size

      The linkage-disequilibrium method (Hill 1981; Waples 2006; Waples & Do 2008): in a small population, drift creates associations between unlinked loci in proportion to 1/(3Ne). The r² expected from sampling alone is subtracted, and the remainder gives Ne of the parental generation. Jackknife over loci for the confidence limits.

      5 · Fine-scale spatial genetic structure

      6 · Mating system

      At inbreeding equilibrium, a selfing rate s produces F = s/(2 − s), so s = 2F/(1 + F). The confidence limits come from a bootstrap over loci of Weir & Cockerham's f.

      7 · PST versus FST

      QST is the FST of a quantitative trait; if it exceeds the neutral FST, populations have diverged more than drift allows — divergent selection. Without a common-garden or crossing design, its phenotypic proxy PST = cσ²B/(cσ²B + 2h²σ²W) is what field measurements can give (Brommer 2011; Leinonen et al. 2013). The confidence limits are exact: they come from the F distribution of the one-way ANOVA (Searle, Casella & McCulloch 1992).
